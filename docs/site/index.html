The obvious answer is wrong

Trips inside the zone were already falling

Compare Uber/Lyft pickups in the 37 tolled zones with 207 zones elsewhere in the city, month by month. If the toll caused a drop, the line should break at January 2025.

The gap shrinks about 8% a year from the start of 2023 and keeps going at the same pace after tolling begins. A simple before/after comparison treats all of that decline as the toll's effect. Economists call this a pre-trend: the treated and comparison groups were already drifting apart before the policy.

The main estimator learns that trend, plus the zone's own seasonal pattern, from the pre-policy months only, then asks how far actual trips fell below that path.

From raw trips to evidence

One billion rows, one laptop

Monthly trip files from the NYC Taxi & Limousine Commission are aggregated with DuckDB into small hourly tables. The tolled zones are identified from the fee actually charged on each trip, not from a hand-drawn map.

Evidence and robustness

Every method points to a small effect

Each estimate is a percentage change in Uber/Lyft pickups inside the zone compared with what the model expects without tolling. Intervals come from resampling whole zones 1,000 times, so correlation within a zone over time is respected.

Forecasting

Tomorrow's demand, hour by hour

Every evening, a LightGBM model forecasts each hour of the next day for the 80 busiest zones. Every input is at least 24 hours old, and tests enforce that rule, so the backtest reflects what the model could really do. Below is one week it never saw during training.

Rolling-origin backtest over April–September 2026: train on everything before a month, forecast that month. WAPE is total absolute error divided by total trips.

Experiment design

How to test a price change when riders share drivers

Randomizing riders breaks down in a marketplace: treated and control riders compete for the same drivers. A switchback randomizes time blocks instead, switching the whole area between treatment and control. The block length is the key decision, so it was tested by injecting a known −2% effect into 12 weeks of real Manhattan demand and replaying 500 random schedules per design.

Recommended design: four-hour blocks, hour-of-week fixed effects and a one-hour burn-in. Even so, power to detect a 2% change in 12 weeks is only about 43%; reaching 80% would take roughly 30 weeks. Running the simulation before launch is what reveals that.
